# Badugi Poker 101: Basics, Rules and Strategies to Make You a Badugi Pro

Yes, it's a triple-draw game that is often found in the mix, and I get some questions about how to play it and what are some of the strategies behind it. ## What Is Badugi?

First of all, what is Badugi and why is it unique? The first thing to know is it's one of the games that you'll find in the poker mix that is actually not poker, because poker is a five-card game and Badugi is a four-card game. So that's one of the first things you'll notice when you're playing Badugi: you're only dealt four cards.

We're talking today about straight Badugi. In future videos we'll talk about how to play it as part of a split-pot mix game like Badeucy or Badacey, but for today we're just talking about regular old Badugi.

## Rule 1: What Makes a Badugi

A Badugi means you have four different suits in your hand — at least a club, a heart, a diamond, and a spade — and you cannot have any pairs. That is critical. Sometimes people see they have a diamond, a club, a heart, and a spade, but they have two of the same numerical value, and that disqualifies you from having a Badugi.

So a Badugi is any four cards that are not paired and are of different suits. The nuts in Badugi is **A-2-3-4**, because it's a lowball game and you're always trying to get the lowest cards of various suits in your hand.

This is what makes Badugi different from other games: you have fewer cards, and you're actually trying to make sure you don't have the same suit in your hand. Another difference: unlike Deuce-to-Seven or Ace-to-Five, where you'll often have to improve your hand unless you were dealt a monster, with Badugi you don't necessarily have to improve in order to still win the pot. That means your starting hand selection is that much more critical.

## Rule 2: Game Format

Badugi is normally played six-handed, although you can play with a few more or fewer. In a normal Badugi game it is **triple draw and limit**.

- Fixed-limit betting (e.g., $20/$40 or $80/$60).
- On the first round and first draw, you bet the smaller denomination ($20).
- On the second and third draws, you bet the larger denomination ($40).
- Triple draw means you get **three chances to improve your hand**.
- You can discard as many cards as you want — even all four. It has been done, but you don't want to be doing that too often if you want to be a winning player.

## Rule 3: You Don't Need a Badugi to Win

You don't actually have to make a Badugi to win. You can have what's called a three-card Badugi, a two-card Badugi, and in theory a one-card Badugi (though I don't know that I've ever seen that in real life).

Any four-card Badugi wins because that's the definition of a Badugi. But let's say you only have three different suits — for example, the **A♣, 3♥, 7♦, K♦**. You only have three different suits because the king and the seven are both diamonds. That's a three-card Badugi: you don't count the king, you just have the ace, the three, and the seven.

If you're against another player who also doesn't have a Badugi, the winner is the one with the best (lowest) three cards of the Badugi suits. If they have an A-4-8 of all different suits, they have an "eight three-card Badugi", and you have a "seven three-card Badugi" — you win because your seven is lower. Always start from the top and work your way down when determining hand strength.

For this reason, your starting cards are very important. If you start with **A-2-3 of different suits**, you're actually a favorite to win even if you don't make the Badugi, because A-2-3 is the lowest three cards you can have that's not a Badugi. Making a Badugi is hard — there are only 13 of each suit — so if your opponents don't make one, you'll win or chop with A-2-3.

The same logic applies even further: if you have just two different suits — say A♠, 2♦ — as long as no one else has three or four different suits, you would likely win that hand as well. That's why it's important to start with low cards of different suits.

## Strategy 1: Position, Position, Position

Like in all poker games, position matters, and being on the button is the best position you can have. In triple-draw games it's even more important.

In Hold'em or Omaha, you get information from betting patterns and reactions. In a triple-draw game, you get **more information** because you get to see how many cards your opponent discards.

- If they **stay pat** (take no cards), they have a very strong hand.
- The only counter-bluff is called **snowing** (covered below).

You get to see this information if you're in position, which lets you decide whether or not to draw. Your opening ranges should widen the better your position. Be in late position when making decisions about starting hands.

## Strategy 2: Starting Cards Really Matter

Your opening range is determined by your position.

- **Early position**: at least three cards of different suits, all seven or below.
- **Late position**: you can play with just two cards in the wheel — A-2, A-3, A-4 — and I would raise in position.

A lot of players in fixed-limit triple-draw games call extra bets thinking, "It's just one more bet to potentially make my hand." Those bets add up. That's the difference between winning and losing players: you have to know when to call an additional bet and when not to. If you start with bad cards, you're going to find yourself drawing dead and in hot water most of the time.

## Strategy 3: Aim for a Seven

A lot of people aim for eights or nines. Eights are okay; I would definitely **not** aim for a nine. Aim as low as possible, because you're going to get one-notched a lot — you make an eight Badugi and someone else has a seven; you make an eight and they have a six. You don't want to get notched out for aiming too high.

A-2 and A-2-3 of different suits are super-strong starting hands. If you're lucky enough to be dealt those, **raise and re-raise** to get opponents out of the pot — you don't want them drawing and potentially making a better Badugi than you.

Once I had A-2-3 in the very first Badugi tournament at the World Series of Poker. On the last draw, I just knew I was golden — we were both drawing one and I had A-2-3. I ended up making a jack Badugi, but my opponent made a ten Badugi and I got knocked out. But in general, A-2-3 is going to get you there more often than not.

## What Is Snowing?

There's an important concept in Badugi called **snowing** — basically bluffing in a triple-draw game. There are times when you want to do it, typically in position so you can see whether your opponent is drawing.

Badugi and other triple-draw games are hands of **card removal**. If you've been dealt A-2-3 with three diamonds in your hand, you know at least two aces and three diamonds are gone — cards your opponents probably need. (I actually wouldn't suggest snowing with those cards, because they're good cards that can win you the pot.)

Let's say you have middle-of-the-road cards: two aces, plus a 7-8, with three diamonds in your hand. Those are cards your opponents likely need. After the first draw, if you pull another six and another ace, you've got great card removal — you know your opponents are likely looking for those cards. Now it's an opportunity to think about whether you want to **stay pat** while opponents are still drawing, representing that you've already made your hand.

You should snow at a fairly decent rate when in position. You'll get called sometimes, but if your bluffs are never being exposed, you're not bluffing enough. When you've got a lot of the cards your opponents likely need, it's a good time to stand pat even if you haven't made your hand and bet on the river — often you'll get a fold because they'll assume you've already made a Badugi.

## Hand Examples

### Example 1 — Improving Your Hand

You're dealt **A♦, 3♥, 9♠, K♠**. (I wouldn't necessarily recommend aiming for a nine, but some would.) Keep the ace, three, and nine; throw away the king.

- **First draw**: 4♠. Although you already had a spade with your nine, you actually improved — now you have A♦, 3♥, 4♠. Throw away the nine. Badugi is a game where you can improve your suit and actually get better.
- **Second draw**: 10♥. Now you have A♦, 3♥, 4♠, 10♥. Still no Badugi, but a strong three-card Badugi. Throw the ten away.
- **Third draw**: 7♣. Now you have **A♦, 3♥, 4♠, 7♣** — a very strong Badugi, likely to win.

If instead you'd blanked and gotten K♥, you still don't have a Badugi, but you still have A-3-4 of three different suits — likely good enough to win.

### Example 2 — Setting Up a Snow

You're dealt **2♠, 4♠, 10♠, J♠** — all spades.

In most cases, throw this hand away. But say you're in the big blind, or on the button in an unraised pot, and somehow you find yourself in the hand. You know that four spades are out of play.

- **First draw**: throw away three cards, keeping just the 2♠. You draw 6♠, 8♠, K♦. Still a terrible hand — but now you've seen **6 of the 13 spades**, and your opponents won't get them. This is an ideal candidate to set up a snow.
- **Second draw**: throw away just one card — the K♦. Keep 2♠, 6♠, 8♠.
- **Third draw**: if you're in position and your opponents are still drawing, **stay pat** and bet. Bet the river too, with the assumption that your opponents won't get the critical spade they need. If they don't have a spade in their hand, they'll fold because they can't make a Badugi — and they'll assume you did, based on how you played.

## Bonus Tips and Closing

That's how you play Badugi. It's a really fun game — give it a shot if you've never played. It's normally played in the mix and is also a part of split-pot games like Badeucy and Badacey. You're not sharing community cards, so it gives a different kind of rush that a lot of people enjoy more than Hold'em in some cases.

You're not going to be playing for no-limit amounts, but you can play in a $200/$400 limit game and the pots can get very hefty if that's your style.
